1. Why this paper matters

  • It is one of the most cited papers in all of social science (the doctor quoted about 192K citations). Most psychology studies of risky driving (speeding, drink driving, phone use, seat belts) use it as their framework.
  • It gives a causal chain from beliefs to behaviour. That chain is the "solid foundation" the doctor wants under the ML model. The ML measures the behaviour; TPB explains why it happens and what can change it.

2. Where it comes from

TPB extends the Theory of Reasoned Action (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975; Ajzen & Fishbein, 1980). In that theory, behaviour follows from intention, and intention follows from attitude and subjective norm. Reasoned Action works only for behaviours under full volitional control: the person can simply decide to do them. Many behaviours are not like that; they also need skill, time, opportunity or other people. Ajzen therefore added a third factor, perceived behavioral control (PBC). This is the "planned" extension.

3. The model

AttitudeIs the behaviour good or bad for me?
Subjective normDo important others want me to?
Perceived behavioral controlCan I do it? Is it up to me?
→
IntentionHow hard I plan to try
→
BehaviourWhat I actually do

PBC also has a direct arrow to behaviour (see section 5).

Intention = w₁·Attitude + w₂·Subjective norm + w₃·PBC
Behaviour = w₄·Intention + w₅·PBC

The weights are not fixed. They are estimated by regression for each behaviour and population. For one behaviour attitude may dominate; for another, social pressure may. This is why you cannot borrow weights from a paper. You estimate them, which is what an SEM replication does.

The three determinants

ConstructAjzen's meaningDriving example (speeding)
Attitude toward the behaviourThe person's overall positive or negative evaluation of performing the behaviour"Speeding on this road is useful / exciting / harmful"
Subjective normPerceived social pressure to perform or not perform it"People important to me think I should keep to the limit"
Perceived behavioral controlPerceived ease or difficulty of performing it, reflecting past experience and expected obstacles"Keeping to the limit is easy for me / up to me"

4. PBC is not locus of control

  • Rotter's locus of control is a general, stable belief that outcomes depend on one's own actions. It is the same across situations.
  • PBC is specific to one behaviour in one context and can change from one behaviour to the next. Ajzen links it most closely to Bandura's self-efficacy (confidence in one's ability to perform a specific behaviour).

5. When PBC predicts behaviour directly

Ajzen gives two reasons for the direct PBC → behaviour arrow:

  1. Motivational. With intention held constant, a person who feels more in control tries harder and persists longer, so they succeed more often.
  2. Proxy for actual control. PBC can stand in for real control, but only to the extent that the perception is accurate. When the person lacks information or the situation is new, PBC predicts behaviour poorly.

Accurate prediction also needs three conditions:

  1. Intention and PBC are measured to match the behaviour (compatibility, section 9).
  2. They stay stable between the time they are measured and the time the behaviour happens.
  3. PBC is realistic.

6. The evidence Ajzen reviewed

The paper reviews earlier empirical studies across many behaviours (not driving-specific). Its headline findings:

  • Attitude, subjective norm and PBC together predict intention with high accuracy. The average multiple correlation is about R ≈ .71 CHECK
  • Intention and PBC together predict behaviour with an average multiple correlation of about R ≈ .51 CHECK
  • Adding PBC improves prediction beyond attitude and subjective norm, which is the empirical case for the "planned" extension.

R = .51 means about 26% of behaviour variance explained (R² = .51²); R = .71 means about 50% of intention variance. Compare this with the Somoray et al. (2024) driving meta-analysis in the companion guide.

7. Beliefs: the deeper layer (expectancy-value)

Each of the three determinants rests on a set of salient beliefs, the beliefs that come to mind most readily:

DeterminantBelief formulaMeaning
AttitudeA ∝ Σ bᵢ·eᵢbᵢ = belief that the behaviour leads to outcome i; eᵢ = evaluation of that outcome
Subjective normSN ∝ Σ nⱼ·mⱼnⱼ = belief that referent j wants me to do it; mⱼ = motivation to comply with j
PBCPBC ∝ Σ cₖ·pₖcₖ = belief that control factor k will be present; pₖ = power of factor k to help or hinder

Why this matters for interventions. To change behaviour you change beliefs. A feedback system cannot change "attitude" directly, but it can change a specific belief such as "harsh braking does not save time". That is the bridge to the gamification / Coach design.

The scaling problem. Ajzen discusses a technical issue: multiplying two scores (b × e) gives results that depend on whether the scales are scored unipolar (1 to 7) or bipolar (−3 to +3). The correlation of the product with other measures changes with that arbitrary choice. Belief-based composites therefore often correlate only moderately with the direct measures. Mention this as a measurement limitation if you ever build a belief-based questionnaire.

8. Is the theory complete? Past behaviour and extra predictors

  • Past behaviour. Past behaviour often predicts future behaviour beyond the TPB variables. Ajzen treats this as a test of the theory's sufficiency. He argues that past behaviour (habit) is not itself a psychological explanation; its residual effect means something relevant is still unmeasured.
  • Open to additions. The theory accepts new predictors if they explain a significant share of variance beyond the three determinants. Examples discussed include personal / moral norms (a feeling of moral obligation) and self-identity. This is why so many driving studies use an "extended TPB": they add, for example, normlessness, risk perception, descriptive norms or past behaviour (see slide 30 of the deck).

9. Measuring TPB correctly

This comes from Ajzen's questionnaire-construction guidance on his TPB website, which is a separate source from the 1991 paper. Read it before you design any questionnaire.

  • Compatibility (TACT). Define the behaviour by Target, Action, Context and Time, and measure every construct at exactly that level. Example: "driving more than 10 km/h above the limit (action) on urban roads in Cairo (context) during the next 4 weeks (time)". A vague behaviour gives weak correlations.
  • Elicitation study first. Ask a small sample open questions (advantages and disadvantages, who approves or disapproves, what makes it easy or hard) to find the salient beliefs. Then write closed items from the most frequent answers.
  • Direct measures. Use several 7-point semantic-differential or Likert items per construct (for example, attitude: bad–good, harmful–beneficial, unpleasant–pleasant). Check internal consistency with Cronbach's α or composite reliability.
  • Behaviour measured later. Measure the behaviour after the questionnaire (prospective design). This is where OBD data fits: it gives an objective behaviour measure instead of self-report.

11. Questions the doctor may ask

QuestionShort answer
Why TPB and not Reasoned Action?Driving behaviours are not fully volitional (traffic, skill, time pressure), which is exactly what PBC adds.
Can OBD measure TPB?OBD measures the behaviour node only. The belief nodes need self-report. Combining the two (questionnaire plus OBD) answers the main criticism of TPB driving studies, which is self-reported behaviour.
What is the intention–behaviour gap?Intention explains only part of behaviour (R ≈ .51 on average in Ajzen's review). The direct PBC path and habit (past behaviour) account for some of the rest.
Is the theory falsifiable?Yes: it predicts which paths are significant and how much variance is explained. These are tested statistically, typically with SEM.
